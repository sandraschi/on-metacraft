# VI. Thresholds

**Status:** Draft v0.1 2026-10-13 (Opus 5.5). New chapter, not in the
Gemini draft. Sources: Reviews C and D, Ch III. Gemini and Sandra review
pending. Not legal or investment advice.
**Revision date:** 2026-10-13.

## Method

The sister books describe each surviving control as an attachment point:
a place where a named party can still apply a brake, with a date for when
it last held. This chapter lists the attachment points that survive the
collapse of reimplementation cost. It is meant to be photocopied.

## The table

| # | Threshold | Who holds it | How it binds | State 2026-10-13 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| A | Patents | incumbents; USPTO, EPO, courts | method claims; independent creation is no defense | partial and decaying: 20-year terms; post-*Alice*; live claims cluster in post-2006 features the clones lack |
| B | Trade dress and trademark | incumbents; courts | look of the product; names and marks | real, cheap to route around: restyle chrome, keep behavior |
| C | Format control | spec owners | undocumented or changing formats | weak where specs are public (PSD, OOXML, PDF 1.7); stronger for DWG history and project formats (AEP, PRPROJ) |
| D | Distribution | Apple, Microsoft, Google stores; code-signing authorities; GitHub | notarization, signing, store policy, takedown | strong: the realistic enforcement chokepoint |
| E | Certification and liability | regulators, insurers, auditors | software that must be certified or audited (medical, avionics, accounting, tax) | strongest: the process, not the output, is audited |
| F | Enterprise bundle | CIOs; the incumbent's cloud | identity, mail, compliance, tenant data, support contracts, indemnity | strong: this is what enterprise software actually sells |
| G | Networks | banks, accountants, payroll and tax systems | the app is a node in a network the clone does not join | strong for QuickBooks-class targets |
| H | Model-provider policy | labs | usage policies; constitutions | variable; outside the clone's control |

Row H is where the Gemini draft's Amodei line belongs: a brake on the
mechanism, held by the lab, not a mechanizer.

## Reading the table

Thresholds A to C are the old moats. They were never strong; labor cost
hid how weak they were. The clones expose them.

Thresholds D to G are not about the program. They are about what
surrounds it: who may ship it, who must certify it, which network it
joins, which bundle it sits in. They are where the incumbents will move,
the way the music industry moved after 2001 from selling copies to
licensing catalogs through a service. The law stopped Napster. It did not
stop file sharing. The industry survived by finding a moat that still
worked.

## Who is exposed

The useful split is not by size or sector but by where the moat sits.

- **Exposed:** vendors whose moat is the application itself: its
  interface, its features, its file format. Desktop creative tools,
  standalone office applications, single-user design and audio tools.
- **Defended:** vendors whose moat is a network, a bundle, data, a
  certification or a liability. Enterprise suites with identity and
  compliance attached; accounting with bank feeds and tax filing;
  certified engineering tools.

Most large vendors are both. Their exposed products are the ones they
sell to individuals and small firms; their defended products are the ones
they sell to enterprises. Expect defection from the bottom first.

## Where the last brake sits

Threshold D deserves one more sentence because it survives every later
chapter. Whoever signs the binary is accountable for it. Swarms can write
code; a code-signing certificate is issued to a named party. When
Chapter IX asks where the human remains in an autonomous software
foundry, the first answer is here.
