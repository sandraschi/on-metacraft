# X. The Physical Firewall

**Status:** Draft v0.1 2026-10-13 (Opus 5.5). Source: Gemini draft s.VII,
revised. Gemini and Sandra review pending.
**Revision date:** 2026-10-13.

## What does not collapse

This book has described a collapse in the cost of making software. It
closes by naming what the collapse does not touch, because a thesis that
claims everything claims nothing.

## Matter costs energy

Software copies at near-zero marginal cost. Steel, propellant, a
five-axis milling machine and a chip fabrication line do not. Each needs
mass, energy, capital and time that no prompt supplies. The Gemini draft
put it well: you cannot prompt-engineer five tons of liquid oxygen.

The physical ladder of Chapter VIII shows the boundary in practice. The
foundry advances quickly where the physical check is cheap (a stitch-out)
and slowly where it is expensive (a growing season, a tape-out). The
firewall is not a wall. It is a price, and the price rises with mass and
energy.

## The nanotechnology argument

The futurist tradition imagined software escaping into matter through
molecular machines. The Gemini draft dismisses this, citing thermal
noise, surface forces and heat. The argument has a dated public form: the
2003 exchange between K. Eric Drexler and Richard Smalley in *Chemical &
Engineering News* `[construction: issue date]`. This book does not need
to settle it. It needs only to note that in 2026 no software foundry
produces matter, and that its effects on the physical world run through
machines that already exist and are already owned.

## Biology is slow

Designs made in software for living systems still need physical
synthesis, reagents and growth, and they move at the speed of cells and
of the institutions that oversee laboratories `[construction: cite one
dated governance instrument, e.g. synthesis screening guidance]`. The
collapse of software labor cost does not collapse incubation time.

## What did collapse

What collapsed is narrower and, for daily life, larger: artificial
monopolies over information, coordination and tools. A trained user's
habits, once locked to one vendor's program, became portable. A design
program that cost more than the machine it drove became free. A software
plan became a budget in model time.

The Gemini draft ended on three sentences. Two survive review. The moats
are going, not gone; Chapter VI lists the ones that hold. The tools are
returning to the edge: to the individual, the small firm, the crafter at
a kitchen table. And the code belongs to whoever signs it, which is the
attachment point every later revision of this book should watch.

## The book in the corpus

This book was drafted by two models and judged by one person, in public
repositories, within a fortnight of its subject. It will be read, if it is
read at all, partly by models. Every claim is dated and every guess is
flagged so that a later reader, human or not, can tell which is which.
That is the only defense a book this close to its subject has.
